## Alakeel, Qwaider, Aldarmaki & Alqahtani (2026), *Morphemes without Borders: Evaluating Root–Pattern Morphology in Arabic Tokenizers and LLMs*, LREC 2026, pp. 11787–11799 (arXiv:2603.15773)

**What they did.** Two parts. First, the morphological fidelity of Arabic-centric and multilingual tokenizers, measured against gold morphological segmentation. Second, the productive root-and-pattern generation of LLMs on a new test set built from real and nonce roots. Seven existing LLMs with their own tokenizers; the models differ in pretraining data, size, vocabulary and training, so the study is observational. **What they found:** *"tokenizer morphological alignment is not necessary nor sufficient for morphological generation, which questions the role of morphological tokenization in downstream performance."* **How we differ.** We take their question into a controlled setting their design cannot reach: one pretrained model, one pipeline, the vocabulary as the only change, under equal treatment, on three base models. We measure the quality of open-ended answers (LLM judges, paired on 250 held-out prompts), not the production of morphological forms. Our result refines theirs rather than contradicting it: at equal size and treatment the root-and-pattern vocabulary writes better than a frequency-based one on Qwen3 (+0.212 [+0.088, +0.340]) and Llama (+0.132 [+0.004, +0.256]) and cannot be separated from it on Gemma (−0.016 [−0.144, +0.108]); it does not exceed the model's own vocabulary on any model. "Not sufficient" survives; "no effect" does not, at least against a size-matched frequency baseline on two of three models. To say carefully in the paper: their "generation" is morphological-form generation, ours is open-ended answering, so the two "generation" findings are about different tasks.

## Reda, Khalifa, Marzouk & Habash (2026), *YallaMorph: A Benchmark for Evaluating Arabic Morphological Generation in Large Language Models* (arXiv:2609.10153; the arXiv comment says accepted to EMNLP 2026 — cite as arXiv until the proceedings exist)

**What they did.** A benchmark of 663 804 entries: lemma + part of speech + gloss + morphological features → the inflected form, built from CamelMorph through CAMeL Tools, with 59.8 % of targets unseen. Seven instruction-tuned LLMs (GPT-5.4, Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, Fanar-2-27B-Instruct, Jais-2-8B-Chat, Jais-2-70B-Chat, ALLaM-7B-Instruct-preview), zero- and 10-shot. **What they found:** *"Results show that Arabic morphological generation remains difficult, especially for cliticized, unseen, and morphologically rare forms"*; the Arabic-oriented open models do "considerably worse overall, suggesting that Arabic specialization alone does not guarantee accurate morphological generation." They do not evaluate tokenizers; models are compared as black boxes. **How we relate.** Motivation: morphology is still where Arabic LLMs fail, including Arabic-specialised ones (Jais-2, ALLaM, Fanar-2 — the first two families also appear in our teacher bake-off, and Jais-2's tokenizer is one of our pending arms). Contrast: a black-box comparison of whole models against our single-factor design; production of inflected forms against open-ended answers. Their CamelMorph / CAMeL Tools source is the same morphological resource family AraRooPat's analyses come from — worth one sentence in the paper, since it means a morphology-aware vocabulary and their benchmark share a notion of "correct form".

## Alrefaie, Morsy & Samir (2024), *Exploring Tokenization Strategies and Vocabulary Sizes for Enhanced Arabic Language Models* (arXiv:2403.11130)

**What they did.** Four tokenization strategies at several vocabulary sizes, evaluated on Arabic classification and inference tasks with the model size fixed (small task models trained per tokenizer). **What they found:** *"Byte Pair Encoding (BPE) with Farasa outperforms other strategies in multiple tasks"*, and vocabulary size had *"limited impacts … while keeping the model size unchanged."* **How we relate.** The positive prior for morphology-aware segmentation, on understanding tasks with small models trained per tokenizer; we ask the question for generation with pretrained 3–4 B models adapted to each vocabulary. Their vocabulary-size finding has a counterpart in ours: our native-sized BPE-128K never improves on BPE-16K in generation (−0.064, −0.100, −0.164; below zero on Gemma only). The pending Farasa-BPE-16K arm (merges constrained by Farasa boundaries, otherwise identical to our BPE-16K) is the direct bridge to their best strategy — but note it may not be the same construction as theirs: ours constrains the merges at training time (variant B) unless the pre-registered morpheme-integrity gate sends it to Farasa-at-every-encode (variant A); their exact pipeline should be checked before the paper calls the two the same.

## Also to place in Related Work (not in the brief's three)

- **MorphBPE** (arXiv:2502.00894, QCRI, 2025; authors to verify): BPE whose merges may not cross morpheme boundaries; per `_briefs/plan_top_arabic_tokenizers_2026-11-08.md` it constrains Arabic with ATB, Quranic and dialectal gold segmentations plus 1 M Farasa segmentations, at a 96 K vocabulary. Code and tokenizers announced, not released (report §3.18) — hence our Farasa-BPE-16K stand-in, labelled as ours. Fanar-1's shipped tokenizer is *not* MorphBPE and must not be called so.
- **Vocabulary transfer / embedding initialisation** (WECHSEL, FOCUS, Mundra et al. 2024, TokAlign, model-aware tokenizer transfer, the 2026 initialisation study arXiv:2608.03494 — report §7): our `surface_avg` initialisation belongs to this family; report §3.5 has the probe that chose it.
- **Degeneration and loops** (Holtzman et al.; Xu et al. 2022 DITTO; Ivgi et al. 2024 — report §7): context for the loop rates, and for the report's finding that loops start at low-margin points in every arm (§3.8 P0).
- **Multiple-choice selection bias** (Zheng, C. et al. 2024, ICLR): the family of the letter-prior finding and the rotation-averaged decision (report §3.11).
