\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

An Arabic word is built from a consonantal root, which carries the core meaning, and a pattern, which makes it a
particular word; clitics attach on either side. \textarabic{بالقلم} (\textit{bi-l-qalam}, `with the pen') is the root
\textarabic{ق-ل-م} in the pattern \textarabic{فَعَل} behind the clitics \textarabic{ب} `with' and \textarabic{ال} `the'.
The tokenizers of current language models learn their units from frequency
\citep{sennrich-etal-2016-neural,kudo-richardson-2018-sentencepiece}, blind to this structure: inside a sentence, an
Arabic BPE of 16\,000 tokens writes \textarabic{بالقلم} as \textarabic{بالق} + \textarabic{لم} and Qwen3's vocabulary
as \textarabic{بال} + \textarabic{ق} + \textarabic{لم}, splitting the root in both. Multilingual vocabularies are also
long on Arabic: on the same texts, the native vocabularies of Qwen3-4B-Base, Llama-3.2-3B and Gemma-3-4B spend 2.30,
2.26 and 2.08 tokens per word, that Arabic BPE 1.50 \citep[fertility;][]{rust-etal-2021-good}. Whether following
morphology helps is unsettled: BPE over Farasa segmentation outperformed other strategies in multiple Arabic tasks with
small models \citep{alrefaie2024exploring}, and MorphBPE \citep{asgari2025morphbpe} keeps BPE merges inside morpheme
boundaries; but across seven LLMs, ``tokenizer morphological alignment is not necessary nor sufficient for
morphological generation'' \citep{alakeel2026morphemes}, and producing inflected forms remains hard for current models,
Arabic-specialised ones included \citep{reda2026yallamorph}.

These studies compare existing models that differ at once in pretraining data, size, vocabulary, instruction data and
decoding \citep{alakeel2026morphemes,reda2026yallamorph}, or train one small task model per tokenizer
\citep{alrefaie2024exploring}. The first design cannot attribute a difference to the tokenizer, and neither asks the
model for an open-ended answer. Yet a tokenizer also acts through its decode side---whether the decoded text is the
intended word, whether the model ends its answer, whether it loops---and this is measured only when the model writes.
What is missing is a single-factor experiment: one pretrained generative model, one pipeline, the vocabulary as the
only change, evaluated on open-ended generation and paired on the same prompts.

We built AraRooPat, a vocabulary of 17\,184 tokens in which a content word is a root token followed by a pattern
token: \textarabic{يكتبون} (\textit{yaktub\=una}, `they write', root \textarabic{ك-ت-ب}) becomes \tok{ROOT}{كتب}
\tok{PAT}{يَفْعُلُونَ}, and \textarabic{بالقلم} becomes \tok{CLITICP}{ب} \tok{CLITICP}{ال} \tok{ROOT}{قلم} \tok{PAT}{فَعَلِ} (patterns
written with the template letters \textarabic{ف-ع-ل}). Clitics and closed-class words are tokens of their own; names
and unanalysable words are spelled in characters between markers. Analyses come from CAMeL Tools
\citep{obeid-etal-2020-camel}; weak radicals (\textarabic{قال} / \textarabic{يقول}, `said / says') are kept as a marked
slot of the root, and the \textarabic{لِ} + \textarabic{الـ} contraction (\textarabic{للولد}, `for the boy') is undone
at encoding and redone at decoding. The decoder rebuilds the inflected stem from the (root, pattern) pair in its most
frequent written spelling: 99.3\,\% of 2\,000 corpus words come back exactly, and our 250 reference answers reach chrF
99.0 with diacritics removed (91.1 with them). Roots stay whole: the root lies inside one token for 41.8\,\% of the
words where that is possible at all (by construction, for words on the root-and-pattern path), against 5.3\,\% for
BPE-16K and 8.8\,\% for Qwen3. The price is length: 3.19 tokens per word, against 1.50 and 2.30.

We hold everything but the vocabulary fixed. A new vocabulary's embedding rows start from the base model's embeddings
of the strings each token stands for \citep{minixhofer-etal-2022-wechsel,dobler-de-melo-2023-focus,mundra2024empirical},
and the model is adapted on 131\,M tokens of filtered Arabic web text and Wikipedia, embeddings first, then all
weights. Every arm, the native one included, is then fine-tuned on the same 45\,000 examples, whose 27\,000 free-form
answers come from one neutral teacher, Aya-Expanse-32B \citep{dang2024ayaexpanse}, chosen in a bake-off on development
prompts \citep[sequence-level distillation;][]{kim-rush-2016-sequence}. Five rules keep the treatment equal: one
teacher; the same answers for every arm; no test prompt ever shown to the teacher; faulty teacher answers dropped, not
repaired; results read against the teacher's own score. Generation is greedy, under one set of stop rules and a
2\,400-character budget, on 250 held-out CIDAR instructions \citep{alyafeai2024cidar} screened for paraphrases of
training prompts. LLM judges score each answer from 1 to 5 against a reference \citep{zheng2023judging}: gpt-oss-120b
(primary; pooled quadratic-weighted $\kappa$ 0.817 with Gemma-4-31B over 24 cells) and Falcon-H1-34B on every model,
Gemma-4-31B and GPT-5.6 also on Qwen3; differences are paired on the same prompts, with 95\,\% bootstrap intervals.
The comparators are the model's own vocabulary and two BPEs trained on AraRooPat's corpus, of 16\,000 tokens
(size-matched) and 128\,000 (native-sized), on Qwen3-4B-Base, Llama-3.2-3B and Gemma-3-4B
\citep{yang2025qwen3,grattafiori2024llama3,gemmateam2025gemma3}.

At equal size and treatment, AraRooPat writes better than BPE-16K on Qwen3 ($+0.212$ [$+0.088$, $+0.340$] judge points)
and Llama ($+0.132$ [$+0.004$, $+0.256$]); on Gemma the two cannot be separated ($-0.016$ [$-0.144$, $+0.108$]). On
Qwen3 the sign holds under all four judges, with intervals above zero under three of them. Against the model's own
vocabulary, AraRooPat cannot be separated on Llama ($-0.004$ [$-0.132$, $+0.120$]) and trails on Qwen3 ($-0.152$
[$-0.284$, $-0.016$]) and Gemma ($-0.284$ [$-0.416$, $-0.152$]). Re-learning a vocabulary costs: BPE-16K trails the
native vocabulary on all three models ($-0.364$, $-0.136$, $-0.268$, every interval below zero), and the native-sized
BPE improves on BPE-16K on none ($-0.064$ to $-0.164$). AraRooPat's answers are stopped as repetition loops
\citep{holtzman2020curious,xu2022learning} less often than BPE-16K's on every model (6.8 vs 15.6\,\%, 12.8 vs
18.4\,\%, 5.6 vs 8.4\,\%), and of the re-learned vocabularies it gives the reference answers the lowest negative
log-likelihood per character on all three (Qwen3: 0.812 nats; BPE-16K 0.907, native 0.811). Morphology-aware
tokenization is thus neither a free lunch nor nothing: against a frequency-based vocabulary of the same size it changes
what the model writes, by an amount that depends on the base model, and only on Llama can it not be separated from the
model's own vocabulary.

Recognition ranks the vocabularies differently. On two letter-scored multiple-choice benchmarks, Arabic exams and
translated MMLU, plain log-likelihood scoring puts the native vocabulary first and AraRooPat last on all three models,
0.013 to 0.046 in accuracy behind BPE-16K, every interval below zero. Part of the gap lies in the answer letters, a
known weak point of multiple-choice scoring \citep{zheng2024large}: AraRooPat spells the letter \textarabic{أ} in three
tokens where the others use one, and on Qwen3 its deficit follows that letter to whichever slot carries it. Averaged
over rotations of the letters (a diagnostic, not the official scoring), the exam gap can no longer be separated from
zero on any model (Qwen3: $-0.001$ [$-0.030$, $+0.027$]), while an MMLU gap of $-0.026$ to $-0.039$ remains, below zero
on Qwen3 and Llama. A judged answer is not scored through option letters, one reason we evaluate generation
(Section~[TBD]).

To compare with tokenizers that Arabic LLMs ship and with a second morphology-aware design, three more tokenizers go
through the identical pipeline on the same three models. Two are released with Arabic LLMs and evaluated as shipped:
Jais-2's byte-level BPE \citep{anwar2026jais2}, with 150\,222 entries in a 150\,272-row table, and Fanar-1-9B's
tokenizer \citep{fanarteam2025fanar}, with 128\,256 tokens, 4\,773 of them containing an Arabic letter. The third,
Farasa-BPE-16K, is ours: a BPE of 16\,000 tokens whose merges may not cross Farasa morpheme boundaries
\citep{abdelali-etal-2016-farasa}, trained exactly as our BPE-16K apart from that constraint; it stands in for MorphBPE,
whose tokenizers are not released, and for the strategy that led in \citet{alrefaie2024exploring} [TBD: variant fixed
by the pre-registered morpheme-integrity gate, F2]. Against BPE-16K it isolates morphological pre-segmentation at equal
size, corpus and trainer [TBD: W1, three models]; against AraRooPat it compares two morphology-aware designs [TBD: W2,
three models]. Each new arm is also compared with the native vocabulary and with BPE-128K, the size-matched pairing for
the released tokenizers; we report the share of the teacher's score it retains and the share of its tokens that
adaptation never or rarely showed the model, and read Fanar-1's small Arabic share beside its differences [TBD: W1--W4
for Jais-2 and Fanar-1, W3--W4 for Farasa-BPE-16K, W5, F1, F3; three models]. Tables keep the groups \textit{released
as shipped} and \textit{trained by us under identical conditions} apart, since only a comparison within the second is
controlled.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A single-factor protocol} for comparing tokenizers on a pretrained generative model: the vocabulary is
  the only change; initialisation, adaptation budget, teacher answers, decoding, prompts and judges are shared under
  five stated rules of equal treatment; every comparison is paired on the same prompts.
  \item \textbf{AraRooPat}, a root-and-pattern vocabulary of 17\,184 tokens whose decoder rebuilds the written word
  (99.3\,\% of corpus words exactly), with its measured trade-off: whole roots on 41.8\,\% of the words where this is
  possible, at 3.19 tokens per word.
  \item \textbf{A three-model result on open-ended generation} with paired intervals under up to four judges: AraRooPat
  writes better than a size-matched BPE on Qwen3 and Llama and cannot be separated from it on Gemma; no re-learned
  vocabulary is above the model's own on any model.
  \item \textbf{Recognition and generation disagree}: on multiple choice AraRooPat trails BPE-16K on all three models,
  and part of that gap is an artefact of how the answer letters are tokenized.
  \item \textbf{A benchmark against released and Farasa-based tokenizers}: Jais-2, Fanar-1 and Farasa-BPE-16K under the
  same conditions [TBD: results].
  \item \textbf{The platform} (pipeline, held-out sets, judge configurations, tables), with the provenance of every
  number documented.
\end{itemize}
