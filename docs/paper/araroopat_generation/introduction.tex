\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Every Arabic word a language model reads or writes passes through a tokenizer that cannot see how the word is built. An
Arabic word is a consonantal root carrying the core meaning, a pattern that makes it a particular word, and clitics on
either side: \textarabic{بالقلم} (\textit{bi-l-qalam}, `with the pen') is the root \textarabic{ق-ل-م} in the pattern
\textarabic{فَعَل} behind the clitics \textarabic{ب} `with' and \textarabic{ال} `the'. The tokenizers of current models
learn their units from frequency
\citep{sennrich-etal-2016-neural,kudo-richardson-2018-sentencepiece,mielke-2021-tokenization} and, inside a sentence,
write \textarabic{بالقلم} as \textarabic{بالق} + \textarabic{لم} (an Arabic BPE of 16\,000 tokens) or as
\textarabic{بال} + \textarabic{ق} + \textarabic{لم} (Qwen3's vocabulary), splitting the root both times. The tokenizer
is the one component of a model fixed before training and never revised, so every cut it makes is paid at every step;
and multilingual vocabularies pay twice on Arabic, spending 2.30, 2.26 and 2.08 tokens per word (Qwen3-4B-Base,
Llama-3.2-3B, Gemma-3-4B) where that Arabic BPE spends 1.50 \citep[fertility;][]{rust-etal-2021-good}, a cost in context
and compute that falls unequally across languages \citep{petrov-2023-unfairness}.

Should a tokenizer follow morphology, then? A long line of evidence says yes. Morphological complexity makes language
modelling harder and linguistically motivated segmentation eases it \citep{park-2021-morphology}; segmentations closer
to morphemes helped a masked model interpret derived words \citep{hofmann-2021-superbizarre}, gave a unigram model an
edge over BPE \citep{bostrom-2020-bpe}, and have been built into tokenizers and models for Kinyarwanda
\citep{nzeyimana-2022-kinyabert}, Turkish \citep{toraman-2023-tokenization} and English
\citep{jabbar-2023-morphpiece,bauwens-2024-bpeknockout,libovicky-2024-lexically}. For Arabic, AraBERT pre-segmented its
text with Farasa \citep{antoun-2020-arabert}, BPE over Farasa segmentation led several tasks with small models
\citep{alrefaie2024exploring}, and MorphBPE, which keeps BPE merges inside morpheme boundaries at training time and
leaves inference unchanged, lowered cross-entropy and improved reading comprehension for Arabic with models trained from
scratch \citep{asgari2025morphbpe,asgari-2026-morphbpe}. The most recent evidence says it may not matter. Six Arabic
tokenizers compared on classification left no single best choice \citep{alyafeai-2023-tokenizers}; across seven LLMs,
``tokenizer morphological alignment is not necessary nor sufficient for morphological generation''
\citep{alakeel2026morphemes}; producing inflected forms remains hard for current models, Arabic-specialised ones
included \citep{reda2026yallamorph}; how consistently a subword vocabulary segments inflected forms is itself in
question \citep{dimarco-2024-subword}; and the handicap of morphologically complex languages has been traced to data
volume rather than to morphology \citep{arnett-2025-morphology}. Meanwhile the tokenizer has been promoted from
preprocessing step to design decision
\citep{rust-etal-2021-good,ali-2024-tokenizer,dagan-2024-tokenizer,alqahtani-2026-tokenizers} without an agreed way to
measure one: compression alone does not predict performance
\citep{goldman-2024-unpacking,schmidt-2024-compression,lotz-2025-beyondcompression}, and intrinsic morphological scores
are still being built \citep{batsuren-2024-alien,uzan-2024-greed}.

Both sides can be right, because neither has isolated the tokenizer, and neither has watched the model write.
Comparisons of existing models differ at once in pretraining data, size, vocabulary, instruction data and decoding
\citep{alakeel2026morphemes,reda2026yallamorph}, so a difference cannot be attributed to the tokenizer; studies that
train one model per tokenizer
\citep{alyafeai-2023-tokenizers,toraman-2023-tokenization,alrefaie2024exploring,asgari-2026-morphbpe}, up to the
fourteen otherwise identical models of TokSuite \citep{altintas-2025-toksuite}, hold the tokenizer as the only variable,
but with models trained from scratch, not with a pretrained generative model; and the line of work that replaces or
extends the vocabulary of a pretrained model --- embedding initialisation
\citep{minixhofer-etal-2022-wechsel,dobler-de-melo-2023-focus,mundra2024empirical}, token alignment and transplantation
\citep{li-2025-tokalign,goddard-2025-omp}, controlled comparisons of adaptation methods
\citep{andriushchenko-2025-tokenizer-russian} and Arabic vocabulary extension
\citep{bari-2025-allam,zhu-2025-arallama,kashirskiy-2025-aratoken} --- asks how to transfer a vocabulary, not which
vocabulary design writes better. The measure is the deeper problem. Almost every number above is accuracy on
classification or multiple choice scored by log-likelihood. Such a score reads a tokenizer through the tokens of its
answer labels --- the letter's own tokenization moves multiple-choice accuracy by up to 11\,\% and reshuffles model
rankings \citep{zheng2024large,sanz-guerrero-2025-mindthegap} --- and the format is a poor proxy for what a model
writes: selecting an option is a different skill from producing an answer \citep{balepur-2025-mcqa}, log-likelihood
scoring misaligns with the model's own generated answers \citep{lyu-2024-beyondprobabilities}, multiple-choice and
open-ended results disagree across models \citep{wei-2024-rethinking}, and free-form answers matched against a reference
measure generative ability where option formats cannot \citep{myrzakhan-2024-openllm,chandak-2025-answermatching}. A
tokenizer's effect on downstream performance is itself stronger on generation than on classification
\citep{goldman-2024-unpacking}, and log-likelihood never touches the half of a tokenizer that acts when the model
writes: whether the decoded text is the intended word, whether the model ends its answer, whether it loops. We therefore
argue that open-ended generation is the best available test of a tokenizer: it is the only measure that exercises both
halves, encoding and decoding, and it is how a user meets one. Scored by reference-guided LLM judges, which track human
preference well \citep{zheng2023judging}, it is also the direction Arabic evaluation has taken
\citep{elfilali-2024-aragen}.

This paper runs that experiment. We built AraRooPat, a vocabulary of 17\,184 tokens in which a content word is a root
token followed by a pattern token: \textarabic{يكتبون} (\textit{yaktubūna}, `they write', root \textarabic{ك-ت-ب})
becomes \tok{ROOT}{كتب} \tok{PAT}{يَفْعُلُونَ}, and \textarabic{بالقلم} becomes \tok{CLITICP}{ب} \tok{CLITICP}{ال}
\tok{ROOT}{قلم} \tok{PAT}{فَعَلِ} (patterns written with the template letters \textarabic{ف-ع-ل}). Clitics and
closed-class words are tokens of their own; names and unanalysable words are spelled in characters between markers;
analyses come from CAMeL Tools \citep{obeid-etal-2020-camel}, weak radicals (\textarabic{قال} / \textarabic{يقول}, `said
/ says') are kept as a marked slot of the root, and the \textarabic{لِ} + \textarabic{الـ} contraction
(\textarabic{للولد}, `for the boy') is undone at encoding and redone at decoding. The decoder rebuilds the inflected
stem from the (root, pattern) pair in its most frequent written spelling: 99.3\,\% of 2\,000 corpus words come back
exactly, and our 250 reference answers reach chrF 99.0 with diacritics removed (91.1 with them). Roots stay whole ---
inside one token for 41.8\,\% of the words where that is possible at all, against 5.3\,\% for BPE-16K and 8.8\,\% for
Qwen3 --- at a price in length: 3.19 tokens per word, against 1.50 and 2.30. Then we hold everything but the vocabulary
fixed. A new vocabulary's embedding rows start from the base model's embeddings of the strings each token stands for
\citep{minixhofer-etal-2022-wechsel,dobler-de-melo-2023-focus,mundra2024empirical}; the model is adapted on 131 M tokens
of filtered Arabic web text and Wikipedia, embeddings first, then all weights; every adapted arm is then fine-tuned on
the same 45\,000 examples, whose 27\,000 free-form answers come from one neutral teacher, Aya-Expanse-32B
\citep{dang2024ayaexpanse}, chosen in a bake-off on development prompts \citep[sequence-level
distillation;][]{kim-rush-2016-sequence}, under five rules of equal treatment: one teacher; the same answers for every
arm; no test prompt ever shown to the teacher; faulty teacher answers dropped, not repaired; results read against the
teacher's own score. The reference is the model as shipped: its own vocabulary and no training of ours. Six vocabularies
go through the pipeline on Qwen3-4B-Base, Llama-3.2-3B and Gemma-3-4B
\citep{yang2025qwen3,grattafiori2024llama3,gemmateam2025gemma3}: two BPEs trained on AraRooPat's corpus, of 16\,000
tokens (size-matched) and 128\,000 (native-sized); AraRooPat; and, in a second round under the identical pipeline, the
tokenizers shipped with two Arabic LLMs --- Jais-2's byte-level BPE \citep{anwar2026jais2}, 150\,222 entries in a
150\,272-row table, and Fanar-1-9B's \citep{fanarteam2025fanar}, 128\,256 tokens of which 4\,773 contain an Arabic
letter --- and a second morphology-aware design of our own, Farasa-BPE-16K, a BPE of 16\,000 tokens whose merges may not
cross Farasa morpheme boundaries \citep{abdelali-etal-2016-farasa}, trained with BPE-16K's corpus and settings:
MorphBPE's recipe, whose tokenizers are not released, and the strategy that led in \citet{alrefaie2024exploring}.

Every arm answers the same 250 held-out CIDAR instructions \citep{alyafeai2024cidar}, screened for paraphrases of
training prompts, by greedy decoding under one set of stop rules and a 2\,400-character budget. An LLM judge then scores
each answer against a human-written reference on a five-point scale \citep{zheng2023judging}: 5 is a correct, complete,
fluent Modern Standard Arabic answer that does exactly what the instruction asks; 4 has minor omissions or small
language errors and is still clearly useful; 3 is partially correct or only partly follows the instruction; 2 is mostly
wrong or off-topic but contains some relevant Arabic; 1 is empty, not Arabic, a repetition loop, gibberish or entirely
off-topic. The judge is told that the reference is a guide and not the only acceptable answer, not to reward length, and
to judge meaning rather than wording; it also returns sub-scores for correctness, fluency and instruction following and
flags for repetition, wrong language, empty, off-topic and truncated answers. The primary judge is gpt-oss-120b (pooled
quadratic-weighted $\kappa$ 0.817 with Gemma-4-31B over 24 cells), with Falcon-H1-34B on every model and Gemma-4-31B and
GPT-5.6 also on Qwen3. A cell's score is the mean of its 250 integer scores; a difference between two arms is the mean
of their per-prompt differences, with a 95\,\% interval from 10\,000 bootstrap resamples of the prompts and the share of
prompts the first arm wins, ties and loses.

Four findings follow, each on three base models.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Morphology helps at equal size, by an amount that depends on the model.} AraRooPat writes better than
  BPE-16K on Qwen3 ($+0.212$ [$+0.088$, $+0.340$] judge points) and Llama ($+0.132$ [$+0.004$, $+0.256$]) and cannot be
  separated from it on Gemma ($-0.016$ [$-0.144$, $+0.108$]); on Qwen3 the sign holds under all four judges, with
  intervals above zero under three. Its answers are stopped as repetition loops
  \citep{holtzman2020curious,xu2022learning} less often than BPE-16K's on every model (6.8 vs 15.6\,\%, 12.8 vs
  18.4\,\%, 5.6 vs 8.4\,\%), and of the adapted vocabularies it gives the reference answers the lowest negative
  log-likelihood per character on all three (Qwen3: 0.812 nats; BPE-16K 0.907). The native-sized BPE-128K improves on
  BPE-16K on no model ($-0.064$ to $-0.164$).
  \item \textbf{Against the model as shipped, AraRooPat comes closest where the shipped model is strong.} Off the shelf,
  Qwen3-4B-Base answers the task well (judge 2.376, loops on 6.8\,\% of answers). AraRooPat is the adapted arm nearest
  to it ($-0.064$ [$-0.200$, $+0.068$]); Farasa-BPE-16K ($-0.128$) and the shipped Jais-2 vocabulary ($-0.132$) also
  stay within their intervals of it, while BPE-16K ($-0.276$ [$-0.428$, $-0.128$]), BPE-128K ($-0.340$ [$-0.492$,
  $-0.188$]) and Fanar-1's vocabulary ($-0.212$ [$-0.344$, $-0.084$]) fall clearly below; under the second judge only
  BPE-128K does. The shipped Llama-3.2-3B and Gemma-3-4B loop on 33\,\% and 55\,\% of their answers, and every adapted
  arm is far above them ($+0.26$ to $+0.77$ under the primary judge; AraRooPat $+0.768$ [$+0.644$, $+0.900$] and
  $+0.412$ [$+0.276$, $+0.548$]); that gap measures what the pipeline gives a model that never learned the task, and is
  read as such.
  \item \textbf{Recognition disagrees with generation, for a reason that is not the vocabulary's quality.} On two
  letter-scored multiple-choice benchmarks, Arabic exams and translated MMLU, log-likelihood scoring puts AraRooPat
  0.013 to 0.046 in accuracy behind BPE-16K on all three models, every interval below zero. AraRooPat spells the answer
  letters in three tokens where the others use one --- the label-tokenization effect
  \citet{sanz-guerrero-2025-mindthegap} measure: averaged over rotations of the letters (a diagnostic, not the official
  scoring), the exam gap can no longer be separated from zero on any model (Qwen3: $-0.001$ [$-0.030$, $+0.027$]), while
  an MMLU gap of $-0.026$ to $-0.039$ remains. A judged answer is not scored through option letters --- the measurement
  argument above, seen in our own numbers.
  \item \textbf{Against the tokenizers Arabic LLMs ship, and against a second morphology-aware design, AraRooPat holds.}
  A pre-registered gate decided how Farasa-BPE-16K runs: with inference unchanged, as in MorphBPE, its merges put only
  65.5\,\% of Farasa's boundaries on token boundaries, below the 0.90 we had set, so the arm segments every input with
  Farasa before encoding (96.0\,\%), at 6.3 µs per word against 3.6 for the same merges on raw text \citep[how a
  vocabulary is applied at inference is a choice of its own;][]{uzan-2024-greed}. The morpheme constraint helped on
  Qwen3 ($+0.148$ [$+0.040$, $+0.264$]) and Llama ($+0.128$ [$+0.008$, $+0.248$]) and not on Gemma ($-0.064$ [$-0.192$,
  $+0.064$]) --- the same model-dependence as AraRooPat's, the second judge agreeing in sign on every model --- and
  Farasa-BPE-16K and AraRooPat end level everywhere (within $\pm$0.07 under either judge). The tokenizer of Jais-2
  trails AraRooPat on all three models ($-0.068$, $-0.160$ [$-0.292$, $-0.024$], $-0.128$ [$-0.248$, $-0.004$]) and
  Fanar-1's on Qwen3 ($-0.148$ [$-0.268$, $-0.028$]) and Llama ($-0.084$), with a tie on Gemma ($+0.020$); every new arm
  matches or beats the native-sized BPE-128K. Two facts frame the shipped tokenizers. Fanar-1's vocabulary is
  Arabic-poor by construction --- 3.7\,\% of its tokens bear an Arabic letter, close to the native vocabularies'
  2.4-$-3.2$\,\% --- and 56\,\% of its tokens never occur in our 131 M adaptation tokens (Jais-2 38\,\%, Farasa-BPE-16K
  3\,\%); it is level with the 16 K designs even so. And the four tokenizers we trained, on a whitespace-collapsed
  corpus, cannot write a line break while the shipped and native ones can: a layout advantage that every
  shipped-versus-ours difference carries and that AraRooPat leads against anyway. Tables keep the groups
  \textit{released as shipped} and \textit{trained by us under identical conditions} apart, since only a comparison
  within the second is controlled.
\end{itemize}

Our contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A single-factor protocol} for comparing tokenizers on a pretrained generative model --- the vocabulary
  is the only change; initialisation, adaptation budget, teacher answers, decoding, prompts and judges are shared under
  five stated rules of equal treatment; every comparison is paired on the same prompts --- together with \textbf{the
  case for open-ended generation as the primary measure of a tokenizer}, backed by our own multiple-choice results.
  \item \textbf{AraRooPat}, a root-and-pattern vocabulary of 17\,184 tokens whose decoder rebuilds the written word
  (99.3\,\% of corpus words exactly), with its measured trade-off: whole roots on 41.8\,\% of the words where this is
  possible, at 3.19 tokens per word.
  \item \textbf{A six-vocabulary, three-model benchmark on open-ended generation} against the models as shipped, with
  paired intervals under up to four judges: morphology helps at equal size on two models of three, AraRooPat comes
  closest to the shipped Qwen3, a Farasa-constrained BPE and AraRooPat are level, and AraRooPat leads the tokenizers
  shipped with Jais-2 and Fanar-1 on five of six model comparisons, three of them with intervals that exclude zero.
  \item \textbf{The platform} --- pipeline, held-out sets, pre-registered readings, judge configurations and tables ---
  with the provenance of every number documented.
\end{itemize}
